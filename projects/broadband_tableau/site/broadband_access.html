Broadband Access & the Digital Divide (California)

A Tableau Public dashboard exploring how poverty aligns with the broadband adoption gap and unserved households across California counties.

Overview & Driving Question

Driving question: How does broadband adoption vary across California counties, and what does the gap between service availability and actual adoption tell us when combined with poverty and demographic characteristics?

California’s statewide unserved rate is relatively low; the adoption gap is where the digital divide appears. I focused on four visuals: a filled map of poverty %, a stacked bar (offered vs. connections), a bubble chart relating poverty to adoption gap (size = connections), and a bar of unserved share. Together, they surface outliers and priority counties.

Interactive Dashboard (Tableau Public)

Headline KPIs

Households Offered vs. Connections

Supply vs. use. Where service exists and the number of households that remain offline or with lower quality internet service.

Adoption Gap (Households)

Total households not adopting despite being offered service.

Broadband Adoption Rate

Connections ÷ Households Offered (statewide).

Data & Sources

California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC)

  • CPUC Fixed Adoption by County (PDF) — households offered, connections, adoption rate.
  • CPUC Served Status by County (25/3) (PDF) — served vs unserved households.

U.S. Census — American Community Survey (ACS)

  • S1701: Poverty Status (5-year, 2023) — % below poverty line.
  • DP05: Demographic & Housing (5-year, 2023) — race/age (optional overlays).

Joins: county-level; County used for readability in Tableau, GEOIDs retained for future QGIS work.

Method & Build Notes

  • Extracted CPUC PDFs (Adoption & Served Status) and converted to a .xlsx file using Excel's "Get Data" tab.
  • Pulled CSV files for the ACS S1701 (for poverty data) and DP05 (for demographics data) datasets from the portal at data.census.gov.
  • Consolidated the CPUC and census data into one file, county_master.csv, with the following columns for eachcounty:
    • All_HH: total number of households
    • Offered: households with access to broadband
    • Connections: those who actually have broadband
    • Adoption_Rate: ratio of Offered / Connections
    • Served/Unserved 25/3: households with (served) and without (unserved) download and download speeds of 25Mbps and 3Mbps up
    • Poverty_Pct: the percentage of those living below the poverty
  • Created calculated measures:
    • Adoption_Gap = Offered − Connections
      • ,
    • Unserved_Share = Unserved / All_HH
      • .
  • Designed four visuals (filled map, stacked bar, bubble, bar) + three KPIs in a blue–teal palette.

Decisions for Visuals

My goal for the visuals was to begin with a broader view of the data (map, KPIs), and use the other visuals to provide more detailed information about the state of broadband adoption. In addition I hope to be able to illustrate where the state could focus future efforts to address the digital divide in the state.

  • Poverty filled map: context for economic hardship.
  • Stacked bar (Offered vs Connections): intuitive view of the adoption gap.
  • Bubble (Poverty × Adoption Gap, size=Connections): surfaces outliers.
  • Bar (Unserved Share): ranks coverage gaps separately from adoption.
  • KPIs: Offered vs Connections, Adoption Gap (hh), Adoption Rate.

Findings & Interpretation

  • Statewide access is high; adoption lags — the divide is behavioral/economic, not purely infrastructural.

      • The behavioral/social explanation is supported by the fairly large gaps that exist in urban counties like Los Angeles and San Francisco Bay Area counties. However, when looking at adoption rate, the counties with the lowest percentage of the population with broadband are all smaller, more rural regions (2020 Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) Codes). There is also a small number of households for which broadband is not offered (16.2 million households, but 15.8 million offered broadband). A further investigation into the characteristics of these households would also be of use.

  • High-gap, low-poverty outliers suggest non-economic barriers (awareness, devices, digital literacy, affordability).
  • High-poverty, high-gap clusters align with equity priorities for targeted outreach and subsidy programs.

Limitations: adoption reflects connections; “availability” reflects advertised tiers (25/3). DP05 retained for future expansion.
