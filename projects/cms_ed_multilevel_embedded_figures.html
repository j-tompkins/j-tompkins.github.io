Emergency Department Throughput Across U.S. Hospitals

Hospitals do not operate in isolation. They are shaped by the state systems around them, including regulation, reimbursement, workforce supply, and healthcare infrastructure. I used CMS Care Compare data to examine whether emergency department throughput differs meaningfully across states after accounting for hospital characteristics.

The Question

Where does variation in ED throughput come from?

Emergency department throughput is often discussed as a hospital performance issue, but hospitals within the same state share policies, labor markets, reimbursement systems, and regional constraints. That means part of the observed variation may exist above the hospital level.

The analysis therefore asked a more specific statistical question: after accounting for hospital type, ownership, and emergency department volume, how much variation remains between states?

The Data

The first step was understanding which hospitals were truly comparable.

The CMS files include acute-care hospitals alongside psychiatric, children’s, federal, long-term care, and other specialized facilities. My original plan was to include every hospital and adjust for type in the model. The data audit showed that this would combine facilities with substantially different patient populations and operating purposes.

I refined the primary population to acute-care and critical-access hospitals, producing a more interpretable comparison of general hospitals providing emergency services. The broader hospital sample was preserved and analyzed separately as a sensitivity check rather than being discarded.

Data audit Found several specialized hospital groups with distinct roles and populations.

The Statistical Strategy

The models were built in stages so that each one answered a different question.

I began with ordinary linear regression to establish a familiar baseline. I then fit an empty random-intercept model to separate state-level variation from hospital-level variation. Finally, I added hospital characteristics to determine whether state differences remained after adjustment.

Why the Hierarchy Matters

Hospitals in the same state share context.

Ordinary regression treats every hospital as independent. That assumption is difficult to justify when hospitals within a state share the same policy environment and regional health system. A multilevel model accounts for this clustering by allowing each state to have its own baseline while estimating the overall hospital-level relationships.

The intraclass correlation coefficient summarizes how much of the total variation is attributable to differences between states. Partial pooling then tempers unstable state estimates, especially when a state has relatively few reporting hospitals.

Model Checks

The analysis did not stop after fitting the model.

Residual and Q–Q plots were used to assess whether the linear mixed model adequately represented the data. The workflow also checks collinearity, heteroscedasticity, residual normality, and singularity. These diagnostics help distinguish a model that runs from one that is credible enough to interpret.
